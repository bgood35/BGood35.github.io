At a recent ANA conference full of thoughtful AI applications, one presenter's pitch for adopting an "AI-first" strategy stopped me cold, creating urgency by stating with confidence and precision that every dollar not invested in AI today would cost an organization $2.18 to catch up next week. As an optimistic AI pragmatist, I see the virtue in integrating AI into one's workflow while still questioning the numbers, but the larger issue is that "AI-first" isn't a strategy. A technology with countless possible uses gives the people executing no direction on *which* problem to solve or *how* — and strategy has to start with the problem, not the tool.

A good strategy creates clarity by aligning teams on how decisions are made, and how resources and technology should be deployed. When developing Integrated Marketing strategies, I interrogate my assumptions and hypotheses against "what must be true" for a successful result. Working with Brand, Insights, Sales, and Product teams, we align context and conditions in which the strategy will be deployed to ensure the necessary guardrails are accounted for. A strategy without such a foundation risks allocating resources against objectives that were never achievable.

While my approach helped me discuss trade-offs and down-stream implications of decisions with brand leads, a recent [post from Professor Koen Pauwels](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/agi-manifesto-parlay-bet-how-actually-show-awareness-pauwels-vdvtc/) helped me sharpen that thinking even further; reviewing a well-known forecast of AI's growth, he demonstrated that plans based on multiple, high probability outcomes are still likely to fail because of the Parlay Fallacy. A parlay bet pays only if every leg hits; if four bets each have an 80% probability of occurring, the probability of the parlay coming to fruition is just 41%. Applying this to my own work, I thought about how AI can be deployed to score the likelihood of a successful business outcome by treating each department as an individual leg in a parlay.

Instead of assuming or omitting inputs from other departments when building Integrated Marketing plans, AI can help leaders scenario plan and reallocate resources when the situation changes. As Scott Galloway has said:

> One of the biggest mistakes in business strategy is thinking your adversaries are speed bags and won't hit back.

— Scott Galloway, ["No Mercy / No Malice"](https://www.profgalloway.com/speed-bag/)

By building an integrated model across departments, organizations can better understand if they need to reallocate resources across divisions that are simultaneously competing for resources and impacted by the decisions made by the other departments.

- Changes to the cost of goods or the inability to ship products on time can greatly impact Marketing and Trade budgets if they are linked to a percentage of sales or profit.
- A competitor starting to compete on price can quickly hurt market share and cash flow, but as [Mark Ritson cautions](https://www.thedrum.com/opinion/mark-ritson-your-next-discount-may-be-your-last-10-reasons-to-never-ever-cut-prices), competing this way can impact your relationships with distributors, retailers, and customers, requiring you to revisit your long-term trade budgets and profitability forecasts.
- Unexpected competitive activity can negatively impact [eSOV assumptions that were used to create your Integrated Marketing plan](/posts/2026-07-30-brand-positioning-is-a-verb/), creating a need for more brand building or conversion support to recruit the expected number of new households.

The purpose of the exercise is not to change how each department builds their respective strategies or plans, but to give leaders confidence in how they manage resources when things ultimately change. The sobering fact is that the more legs a parlay includes, the worse the odds get, even as the ability to forecast each underlying event improves.

While AI can price the odds based on what's known, it can't see around corners. But the goal isn't to land on a precise number, which would be impossible to accurately forecast in a dynamic environment filled with unknowns. The goal is to create an accurate representation of the playing field to uncover the path that would produce the greatest competitive advantage, reducing the risk in resourcing decisions. Rather than seeking ways for AI to replace the judgment of leaders and specialists, the bigger opportunity is to find novel ways to augment it.
