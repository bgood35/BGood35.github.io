After walking through how six leading thinkers — Sharp, Ritson, Sutherland, Veselinovic, Joachimsthaler, and the Porter–Martin–Barney strategy tradition — approached brand positioning from different perspectives, [Paul Syng put a firm stake in the ground by identifying how a position is built: by repeating costly, hard-to-copy decisions that wire themselves into a buyer's memory](https://paulsyng.com/blog/six-doors-one-room/).

Agreeing with Syng that a brand position is wired through costly decisions and fades without them, I wanted to build on his foundation with a framework that helps marketers defend marketing investment under a CFO's scrutiny. Because a brand position is an asset that must be consistently funded to hold or grow its value, it should be treated less like ad copy and more like a risk assessment that accounts for the dynamic landscape in which brands compete.

If that argument sounds familiar to the case being made for AI investment, it's because it is. [There is data-backed research from Ehrenberg-Bass on what happens when brands stop advertising;](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/what-happens-when-brands-stop-advertising) the AI version has no such evidence. I've seen several presentations cite a claim that "every dollar you don't spend on AI today will cost two dollars next week," but I have not been able to source it, and I'd welcome the citation if it exists. This isn't to say brands shouldn't invest in AI, but a CFO weighing capital for marketing or AI needs to understand the long-term opportunity cost of both and assess the risk on a level playing field.

Syng notes that the real work belongs to the operator, and that an outsider can at most help read the decisions a company has already made. The accompanying deck is that reading instrument: it arranges the six perspectives on a spectrum by how deeply a position sits within the organization. By identifying where the position lives, the operator can better articulate integrated marketing's Job-to-be-Done, the signal it should be held accountable for, and the risk of underinvesting in the position in CFO-friendly terms.

To be clear, this is not a departure from audience-led integrated marketing. While the framework focuses on marketing and business outputs, it is still on marketers to ensure there are enough real buyers to support the business case. If the business target isn't achievable within the audience universe that marketing's Job-to-be-Done is defined against, the whole exercise is moot.
