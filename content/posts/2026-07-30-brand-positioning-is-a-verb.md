After walking through how six leading thinkers — Sharp, Ritson, Sutherland, Veselinovic, Joachimsthaler, and the Porter–Martin–Barney strategy tradition — approached brand positioning from different perspectives, [Paul Syng put a firm stake in the ground by identifying how a position is built: by repeating costly, hard-to-copy decisions that wire themselves into a buyer's memory](https://paulsyng.com/blog/six-doors-one-room/).

Agreeing with Syng that a brand position is wired through costly decisions and fades without them, I wanted to build on his foundation with a framework that helps marketers defend marketing investment under a CFO's scrutiny. Because a brand position is an asset that must be consistently funded to hold or grow its value, it should be treated less like ad copy and more like a risk assessment that accounts for the dynamic landscape in which brands compete.

If that argument sounds familiar to the case being made for AI investment, it's because it is. [There is data-backed research from Ehrenberg-Bass on what happens when brands stop advertising;](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/what-happens-when-brands-stop-advertising) the AI version has no such evidence. I've seen several presentations cite a claim that "every dollar you don't spend on AI today will cost two dollars next week," but I have not been able to source it, and I'd welcome the citation if it exists. This isn't to say brands shouldn't invest in AI, but a CFO weighing capital for marketing or AI needs to understand the long-term opportunity cost of both and assess the risk on a level playing field.

Syng notes that the real work belongs to the operator, and that an outsider can at most help read the decisions a company has already made. The accompanying deck is that reading instrument: it arranges the six perspectives on a spectrum by how deeply a position sits within the organization. By identifying where the position lives, the operator can better articulate integrated marketing's Job-to-be-Done, the signal it should be held accountable for, and the risk of underinvesting in the position in CFO-friendly terms.

![How the six doors translate into a single framework](/images/doors-to-framework-translation-2026.07.30.png)

To be clear, this is not a departure from audience-led integrated marketing. While the framework focuses on marketing and business outputs, it is still on marketers to ensure there are enough real buyers to support the business case. If the business target isn't achievable within the audience universe that marketing's Job-to-be-Done is defined against, the whole exercise is moot.

<h4>1. Brand Positioning is a Verb</h4>
<p>Treating a brand position as a dynamic financial asset that needs to be supported, and assessing the risk of underinvesting in it based on marketplace conditions. — Brandon Goodman, July 2026.</p>

<h4>2. Five things settled. One thing not.</h4>
<p>Paul Syng argued in his June 28th essay that Ritson and Sharp agreed about much when they shared the stage in Cannes, but left the argument about positioning unsettled.</p>
<p>Settled: (1) Consistency is criminally undervalued. (2) Mental availability matters most. (3) Distinctive brand assets are non-negotiable. (4) Sophisticated mass marketing beats niche thinking. (5) Brand purpose is largely nonsense.</p>
<p>Not settled — Positioning: the conversation around consistency didn't address brand positioning, a prerequisite for consistency. Positioning remains a point of disagreement between Sharp (distinctiveness; be recognizable) and Ritson (distinctiveness plus relative differentiation). Syng named the mechanism: positions are wired by costly, hard-to-copy decisions. This deck is intended to create a framework marketers can use to (1) demonstrate how brand positioning is foundational to maintaining a consistent brand signal, (2) explore how the depth at which the brand position is embedded into the organization impacts integrated marketing's Job-to-be-Done, and (3) create a framework to help marketers better defend marketing investments to the CFO.</p>

<h4>3. Brand positioning's value has been identified and articulated through research that has nothing to do with marketing</h4>
<p>Five fields, five findings — none from a practitioner selling a solution:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>George Miller (1956)</strong>, psycholinguistics: working memory holds ~7 things; the useful number is 3–5. You compete for one of a few slots, not for attention.</li>
<li><strong>Eleanor Rosch (1970s)</strong>, anthropological fieldwork on color naming among the Dani of New Guinea: categories have centers, not edges. Buyers reach for what sits closest to the center — own the center, own the category.</li>
<li><strong>Tversky &amp; Kahneman (1970s)</strong>, judgment under uncertainty: what comes to mind easily feels more likely, more right. Fluency does work the product can't.</li>
<li><strong>Chamberlin &amp; Robinson (1933)</strong>, price theory: small differences create little monopolies in the buyer's mind, and they get competed away unless something keeps rivals out.</li>
<li><strong>Donald Hebb (1949)</strong>, neuropsychology: cells that fire together wire together; when the firing stops, the connection fades — the hinge for everything that follows.</li>
</ul>

<h4>4. Marketing research has measured the impact of Hebb's findings, but misunderstood it as a capacity problem and not a "wiring" one</h4>
<p>Every message you add competes with the one you already paid to wire. The finding: more messages, less recall. Marketing research supports Hebb's finding that adding messages to a campaign degrades recall of all of them, but the field interpreted this as a capacity problem — a storage limit — rather than a wiring one.</p>
<p>Why the distinction matters: the brain doesn't average claims, it gets confused by them. A capacity problem would require marketers to prioritize a message; the dilution problem requires marketers to ensure each message supports the brand association they are trying to reinforce — each new message weakens the existing ones. (Chart: effectiveness of message recall falls sharply as more messages are added to a single asset. Source: Kantar Millward Brown, Link™ database analysis, as reported in industry coverage; underlying study not publicly published.)</p>

<h4>5. Brand positioning is built through repetition of a pattern in how the organization operates, external marketing efforts, or both</h4>
<p>A brand position becomes a competitive advantage when it builds memory structures rivals can't easily or effectively copy. It is an asset that lives off the balance sheet.</p>
<p><strong>Embedded in the organization (consistency in decisions):</strong> load-bearing, with significant downstream effects across divisions; investments are significant and expensive to copy due to the opportunity cost of dollars and resources; decisions forgo opportunities that would drive incremental revenue but negatively impact the core position. Internal decisions influenced by a brand position are not always perceived by consumers or the marketplace at large.</p>
<p><strong>Applied to an organization (consistency in exposure):</strong> not load-bearing, decisions lack staying power and can be quickly reconsidered without disrupting the business; the opportunity cost of dollars can be significant, but resource allocation carries less of one; brands can easily "test into" different positions. These are perceived by consumers and the marketplace by design, but can have little to no impact on how the business operates.</p>
<p>Cost is the separator — three principles showing why embedded decisions defend a position better than leased tactics: Michael Spence (1973, Nobel 2001) — a signal is only believed when it's expensive enough that a weaker player couldn't afford to fake it. Thomas Schelling (1960) — a commitment is credible precisely because it's costly to reverse. Amotz Zahavi (1975) — the peacock's tail is trusted because a weak bird couldn't afford to grow one.</p>

<h4>6–7. Six practitioners, six depths of position</h4>
<p>Paul Syng argued that practitioners were surrounding the topic of positioning, but their perspectives explored its merits at varying levels of depth. Their arguments stack based on how deeply the brand position is embedded in the organization — the deeper it sits, the more difficult it is for competitors to copy, for brands to change, and for consumers to perceive:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Byron Sharp — Distinction</strong> (not load-bearing): the brand that comes to mind first sells the most, backed by a wall of data; difference is hard to find, easy to lose, and rarely moves volume. Unaccounted for: critical to build brand salience through associations, but no perspective on how to build new ones.</li>
<li><strong>Rory Sutherland — Perception</strong>: price, imagery, and experience can quickly change value perception. Unaccounted for: builds positioning but doesn't defend it.</li>
<li><strong>Mark Ritson — Differentiation</strong>: helps a brand stand out on the shelf and can strengthen the value proposition (pricing power or consumer preference). Unaccounted for: states that meaning is worth having and defending, but not how to build it.</li>
<li><strong>Jelena Veselinovic — Product</strong>: a product needs a reason to exist before it can be successfully promoted and scaled. Unaccounted for: a reason, feature, or need state alone isn't a moat against competition.</li>
<li><strong>Porter · Martin · Barney — Business strategy</strong>: focus on the system, not a plan, claim, or tactic — a system is what a rival can't easily copy; advantage persists when the path to an execution can't be retraced and replicated cheaply. Unaccounted for: not buyer-centric, focused on how a firm operates.</li>
<li><strong>Erich Joachimsthaler — Demand space</strong> (very load-bearing): companies that let category penetration define their KPIs are measuring inside a market rather than choosing one; the demand space shapes the offering and confirms whether sufficient demand exists. Unaccounted for: naming a new demand space invites competitors into the category.</li>
</ul>

<h4>8–9. The depth of a position informs integrated marketing's Job-to-be-Done</h4>
<p>The phase and strength of a brand position are not static; they change over time due to internal and external factors. Across the six depths — demand space, business strategy, product, differentiation, perception, and distinction — the framework maps real-world examples (OpenAI's category-defining ChatGPT; Costco's membership-funded markup cap; David's ingredient competitors can't use; Volvo's decades of safety engineering; Prime's celebrity-driven but unsustained hype; Coca-Cola's century of ubiquity), the audience each depth speaks to, how positioning shows up, how it's communicated, the marketing signal it should be held accountable for (from aided brand awareness at the demand-space level to unaided brand awareness at the distinction level), and the business outcome it drives (from new buyers alone to new buyers plus product velocity).</p>
<p>Marketing should always recruit new buyers, which assumes the demand space is real — a well-defended position is worth nothing if too few buyers value what it owns. Secondary KPIs come into play when marketing must manufacture a competitive advantage in consumers' minds.</p>
<p>High load, hard to copy, slow to decay: demand space and business strategy. Low load, copyable, fast to decay: differentiation and perception.</p>

<h4>10. Data inputs that inform the risk associated with cutting marketing spend</h4>
<p>Advertising can't save every brand, but sales typically erode when a brand stops advertising — and larger brands, likely carrying more "wires," lose momentum more slowly. Three inputs to assess:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Wire dependence</strong> — how much of your price leans on perception rather than the product? Does branding impact brand preference (taste test)? Does marketing impact price sensitivity (share of volume on promotion vs. base) or discourage switching when competitors run promotions? What's the repeat rate of buyers acquired with zero media exposure?</li>
<li><strong>Decay rate</strong> — how fast does the wire fade once you stop firing it? Do purchase cycles grow longer when the brand goes dark, compared to the category overall? How quickly does the brand lose unaided awareness and consideration gained from media exposure?</li>
<li><strong>Replacement speed</strong> — how fast can a funded rival occupy your space? What would competitors' expected excess share-of-voice be if investment is reduced? How much does the category typically invest in marketing relative to revenue, and are competitors positioned to invest more if they see you've cut spending? How many competitors could credibly carry your brand positioning, eroding yours?</li>
</ul>
<p>(Chart: average change in sales by brand size after ad spend stops — small, medium, and large brands all decline, but large brands lose momentum more slowly. Source: Ehrenberg-Bass Institute / Adelaide University, "What happens when brands stop advertising?" (Gelzinis, Kennedy, Beal, Hartnett &amp; Sharp) — 57 cases of brands cutting all mass-media spend in one Australian consumer-goods category over 20+ years; single-category evidence, generalisability still being tested.)</p>

<h4>11. Budget cuts are free to finance because they consider a position to be static; brand positioning is dynamic</h4>
<p>Marketing's impact does not depreciate on a predictable schedule like other capital assets — it's determined by known and unknown marketplace factors. Plotting category loudness against how load-bearing the position is:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>High risk — mindshare contest</strong> (Volvo, David, OpenAI as it actually played out): you risk losing your slot to other brands, but not the meaning; losing the slot is recoverable, since the proof is still there when you come back.</li>
<li><strong>Very high risk — prisoner's dilemma</strong> (Prime, Coca-Cola): marketing IS the position, and rivals are funded, creating an excess-share-of-voice prisoner's dilemma.</li>
<li><strong>Very low risk — foundation is your defense</strong> (Costco, OpenAI as originally assumed): proof fires without marketing, nobody's shouting; a budget cut can hinder discovery but does not weaken your defenses.</li>
<li><strong>Low risk — assumes the status quo holds</strong> (Liquid Death): holding a perception position cheaply because nobody is contesting it — cuts assume new or existing competitors aren't going to enter the space once you've left it.</li>
</ul>
<p>The map moves: OpenAI's risk changed as its position shifted. Assumed in 2023: a demand space so costly to enter that capital was the moat, placing it at very low risk. Actual by 2026: well-funded rivals matched the capability and competed on other layers — business strategy, distribution — once cost-of-entry stopped being a deterrent.</p>

<h4>12. Assessing the long-term risk of a marketing cut based on brand positioning</h4>
<p>Risk ≈ Wire Dependence (how often the proof would have fired) × Decay Rate (whether anything fires without media) × Replacement Speed (how fast a rival can take the slot).</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Very high risk — an exit decision:</strong> you're assuming competitors won't attempt to steal your share of sales by moving into your brand position; you're effectively deciding to exit a category. Impact measured in weeks.</li>
<li><strong>High risk — a capital decision (asymmetric):</strong> you're assuming rivals can't take your position, but winning it back costs more than the original build; the cut is cheap, the reversal is not. Impact measured in quarters, recovery in years.</li>
<li><strong>Low risk — a capital decision (recoverable):</strong> you're assuming competitors won't aggressively move into your brand position based on past performance; the saving is real, and so is the depreciation — monitor category activity closely. Impact measured in quarters.</li>
<li><strong>Very low risk — a cost decision:</strong> the market rewards brands that own a unique space or have a unique proposition; the capability of the product is the marketing. The saving is close to real. Impact measured in years.</li>
</ul>

<h4>13. Brand positions become competitive advantages when they are well defended and cannot be easily (or cheaply) copied by rivals</h4>
<ol>
<li>Being consistent is a product of having a strong brand position. This can be deeply embedded into the organization (and potentially hard to perceive from the outside) or a product of consistent external exposures through marketing investment.</li>
<li>The role of integrated marketing can change based on how deeply embedded a brand's position is. The more deeply embedded, the easier it is for integrated marketing to defend it and the harder it is for the organization to change it.</li>
<li>How deeply embedded a brand's position is in the marketplace informs the level of risk to the business if it isn't sufficiently supported. A brand position is not a static state, so the level of risk will change over time as marketplace factors evolve.</li>
</ol>
