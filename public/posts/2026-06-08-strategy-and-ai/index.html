Strategy Cannot Survive a Polluted Culture

June 8, 2026 · 3 min · Brandon Goodman

“Show me the incentive and I’ll show you the outcome.” — Charlie Munger

In the “who eats whom” debate between Strategy, Execution, or Culture, I would argue that Strategy “eats” Execution and Culture is the water in which everyone swims. Strategy is not a by-product of culture, but it does determine which strategies can survive within an organization. Rather than putting a premium on strategy, where A.I. has become a mandate, it has polluted waters in which strategic thinking previously flourished.

The logic behind aggressive A.I. adoption is understandable. The perceived benefits are real. But the execution has followed a predictable pattern of flawed assumptions, each compounding the last.

The first assumption is that more data collection leads to better answers. In practice, it tends to produce a streetlight effect — organizations put too much emphasis on metrics that are easily and quickly measurable rather than what is meaningful. Without a clear hypothesis and learning agenda, data gets collected in the absence of a well-defined problem. The data answers questions no one thought carefully enough to ask.

The second assumption is that more data improves A.I. outputs. It often does the opposite. Research consistently shows that an excess of data increases decision-making uncertainty, and that correlations between unrelated variables — spurious by nature — become easier to mistake for insight. Volume is not a substitute for signal.

The third assumption is that A.I.-driven efficiency compounds over time into smarter decisions. Jevons paradox complicates this: when a resource becomes more efficient, consumption of that resource tends to rise rather than fall. A.I. is no exception. Operational costs can exceed the human capital they were meant to replace. A.I. can generate countless iterations of a creative asset, but legal and compliance review doesn’t scale with production volume — meaning fewer people are responsible for reviewing more output, not less. This doesn’t just shift creative and strategic work away from humans — it can erode morale in the process.

Underlying all three assumptions is a more fundamental misunderstanding of what A.I. can currently do. Yann LeCun, one of the field’s most respected researchers, has argued that today’s large language models lack the ability to predict the consequences of their actions or to plan sequences toward a goal — because inference, at its core, is next-token prediction. His framework for what a genuinely agentic system would require is architecturally different from anything LLMs currently offer.

None of this is an argument against A.I. It is an argument against deploying it as an end in itself. When adoption becomes the objective, organizations fall into the trap Goodhart’s Law describes — the measure becomes the target, and the target loses its meaning. A.I. is most powerful when strategy precedes it: when the problem is defined, the hypothesis is clear, and the tool is chosen because it fits within a process and improves it, not because it was mandated.
