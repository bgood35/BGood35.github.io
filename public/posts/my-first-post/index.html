Why Marketers Will Get A.I. Wrong Before They Get It Right

A.I. won't fix marketing's measurement problems — but it will expose them. The real challenge isn't learning to prompt; it's learning to think.
May 22, 2026 · 2 min · Brandon Goodman

A.I. is an amazing tool that will make marketers more effective — after we spend months using it in ways that make marketing less effective. We’ve learned that optimizing towards ROAS or Engagement Rates can hurt brand growth despite improved attribution, yet we believe A.I. can overcome this without addressing the underlying gaps in the data. Rather than bolting A.I. onto our current ways-of-working, we need to imagine how we’d build those ways-of-working if A.I. capabilities were always there.

Leaders who position A.I. as the answer without properly wrestling with the question risk sabotaging its long-term potential. While its familiar chat interface made it easier for people to engage with the technology, this positioning created several problems:

  • The answer engine narrative created unrealistic expectations, making it easier for people to root against it and amplify examples of mistakes or hallucinations.
  • Incentivizing organizational “usage” over outcomes encouraged people to find easy ways to incorporate A.I. into existing workflows with smaller, repeatable gains, rather than tackling bigger structural issues.
  • Centering the experience on a chat interface narrowed how people thought about A.I. applications, shifting focus to prompting rather than problem-solving.

The last issue creates the biggest risk for marketing teams, as it takes focus away from the actual job of marketing. Having spent over a decade at media agencies during the shift to digital, I saw how a focus on platform fluency and media metrics gradually eroded organizations’ ability to use media to solve business problems. Agencies that deprioritized strategic training were caught flatfooted as platforms evolved and began engaging clients directly. The risk with A.I. is familiar — mistake tactical fluency for strategy and you’ve lost your competitive advantage.

Building a weekly meal planner for my family, I found that lessons on crafting better prompts were genuinely useful — but as a tactical improvement, not a strategic one. The more valuable lessons came through building the workflow itself: learning what A.I. can access independently versus what must be explicitly provided, where platform integrations begin and end, and how workflow complexity quietly compounds into real resource and cost constraints.

I’m excited to see where A.I. is applied, and I believe it will be most effective when humans stay in the loop. For that to be true, we need to train ourselves to understand how and when to deploy A.I. judiciously — rather than treating it as a hammer and every problem as a nail.
